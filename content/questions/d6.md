# Domain 6 – Stakeholder Communication & Lifecycle Management · Question bank (original practice items, not real exam questions)

## D6-001
objective: 6.1
difficulty: Easy
select: 1
scenario: A stakeholder says, "We want the claims assistant to feel seamless."
question: What is the architect's best next move?
A: Record seamless as a requirement in the specification and move on to design.
B: Propose a streaming chat interface, since streaming makes responses feel seamless.
C: Ask what would make it feel not seamless, and turn the answers into constraints.
D: Ask the stakeholder to send a written definition of seamless after the call.
answer: C
why A: A preference isn't a requirement; there's nothing yet to design against.
why B: A solution proposed before translation skips discovery.
why C: Correct. Translation turns an experience word into bounded, testable constraints such as a latency budget, a handoff rule and a failure path.
why D: Pushing the work back to the stakeholder loses the live elicitation.
lesson: A stated preference signals that more questions are needed.
source: M4 Discovery – translation

## D6-002
objective: 6.1
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: Partway through a discovery call with a hospital network, the architect proposes an augmented pattern for drafting clinical notes. The stakeholder mentions "a quick check" and the architect moves on to design.
question: What discovery failure occurred?
A: The architect should have proposed an agent pattern instead of an augmented call.
B: The architect should have priced the solution before discussing the workflow.
C: The architect should have chosen a delivery route before the pattern.
D: Design began before must-not and must-prove questions; the quick check went unexplored.
answer: D
why A: The pattern choice isn't the failure; incomplete discovery is.
why B: Pricing comes after the constraints are known.
why C: Route selection depends on constraints that weren't gathered.
why D: Correct. A plausible sketch ended the questioning, so PHI handling, the authorisation gate and retention rules surfaced late.
lesson: Finish the four question buckets before proposing anything; chase down every "it's just a review".
source: M4 "The discovery call that turned into a design session"

## D6-003
objective: 6.1
difficulty: Medium

scenario: You are running structured discovery for a regulated lending workflow.
question: Which TWO question areas do stakeholders most often fail to volunteer, so the architect must ask explicitly? (Select 2)
A: What the system must not do (prohibited actions, cases that must go to a human).
B: What the system must do in business terms.
C: What the system must prove (audit and evidence obligations).
D: Which UI colour scheme they prefer.
E: The name of the project.
answer: A,C
why A: Correct. Boundaries and human-routing cases are rarely volunteered.
why B: Stakeholders usually lead with this.
why C: Correct. Proof obligations are cheaper to find in discovery than in legal review.
why D: Not an architectural requirement.
why E: Not a requirement.
lesson: Ask "must not" and "must prove" explicitly.
source: M4 Four questions turn discovery into requirements

## D6-004
objective: 6.1
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: In discovery, a stakeholder says "It just needs to read the form and route it." The routing rules exist in an operations rules engine.
question: How should this be captured in the translation table?
A: Routing may be a deterministic rule: keep it in the engine; confirm owner.
B: Have Claude perform both extraction and routing to simplify the overall design.
C: Record read-and-route as the requirement and move on without further analysis.
D: Replace the rules engine with an agent that learns routing decisions over time.
answer: A
why A: Correct. Each row records the statement, implied constraint, architectural decision and labelled assumption.
why B: That over-assigns deterministic routing to the model.
why C: This skips translation.
why D: There's no reason to replace a reliable existing system.
lesson: One row per item, with the assumption labelled and an owner to confirm it.

## D6-005
objective: 6.1
difficulty: Hard
select: 1
scenario: Two weeks into a build, the partner mentions they process 800 contracts a day, some 300 pages long, and need results in under 30 seconds.
question: Which statement best reflects how these facts should have been handled?
A: Volume alone determines whether 30-second latency is achievable at 800 contracts a day.
B: The build plan is unaffected, because the model's capability was already confirmed.
C: They were feasibility inputs to gather first; the tier's window decides chunking.
D: 300-page contracts make the project infeasible on any current model tier.
answer: C
why A: At about one request every 108 seconds, latency depends on task complexity, model and output length rather than volume.
why B: Capability isn't feasibility; the commitment came too early.
why C: Correct. The constraints feed the verdict, and the window capacity is part of the tier decision.
why D: Large-context tiers or chunking can handle long documents.
lesson: The verdict is only as sound as the constraints gathered before it.
source: M2 "The scoping call that skipped the constraints"

## D6-006

scenario: An architect presents a context-strategy recommendation, explaining what it gains and what it gives up. The CTO approves. Six weeks later, the production bill surprises the CTO.
question: Which element was missing from the trade-off presentation?
A: The model's published benchmark scores for the recommended configuration.
B: The reversal cost, and the per-call figure at production volume.
C: An architecture diagram showing both context strategies side by side.
D: The names of the engineers responsible for each part of the design.
answer: B
why A: Benchmarks don't show business consequence.
why B: Correct. Gain, give-up and reversal cost; the reversal cost and the business-unit translation change the decision.
why C: A diagram doesn't convey the cost consequence.
why D: Irrelevant.
lesson: Every trade-off presentation needs gain, give-up and reversal cost.

question: Which option should be recommended, and what is Option 2 missing?

## D6-011

scenario: Over weeks 4 to 7, an eval score drifted down while error rate stayed flat. No review happened until the quarterly meeting in week 12.
question: What was missing?
A: More dashboards showing the eval score from different angles.
B: A higher error-rate alert threshold to catch quality problems.
C: A governance rule mapping slow drift to a trigger and owner.
D: A larger eval dataset with more examples per category.
answer: C
why A: The signals already existed; nothing decided they mattered.

## D6-020
objective: 6.5
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: On day three of production, a 529 overloaded error at peak took the whole workflow down. The design has no retry logic, fallback or circuit breaker.
question: Which reliability design is correct, including where each control sits?
A: A circuit breaker defined inside the prompt that tells the model to stop when errors occur.
B: Retries in the orchestration layer only, so failed workflows restart from the beginning.
C: Backoff retries at the API call, a breaker at the service boundary, fallbacks in orchestration.
D: A fallback to a cached response for every request by default, so users always get an answer.
answer: C
why A: Circuit breakers sit at service boundaries, not in prompts.
why B: Retries belong close to the API call; fallbacks go in orchestration.
why C: Correct. Each control has its proper layer, and placing it wrong protects the wrong part.
why D: Cached responses as a default may be wrong for live content; fallbacks should be tested and deliberate.
lesson: Reliability controls work only at the right layer.
source: M2 Reliability controls

## D6-021
objective: 6.5
difficulty: Hard
select: 1
scenario: A deployment's monitoring shows a persistent SLA miss. Prompt and configuration fixes have failed twice, and the partner has since tripled volume and added an EU residency requirement.
question: What lifecycle decision is most appropriate?
A: Keep iterating on the prompt until the SLA is met, since fixes are cheaper.
B: Hand the system to the operations team as is and let them tune it.
C: Remove the SLA, since it no longer reflects how the system is used.
D: Re-architect: the constraints have changed, so return to the design phase.
answer: D
why A: Repeated failures and changed constraints point beyond prompt fixes.
why B: Handing off a system that breaches its SLA transfers the problem.
why C: That hides the problem from the business.
why D: Correct (decision rule inferred). When the constraints themselves change, iteration within the old design isn't enough.
lesson: Know which lifecycle phase you're in, and when monitoring should send you back to design.
source: M4 Lifecycle; feedback loop (iterate vs. re-architect); INFERENCE
