## D4-001
objective: 4.1
difficulty: Easy
select: 1
scenario: A team sets its extraction accuracy threshold at 87% because that's what the first prototype achieved.
question: What is wrong with this approach?
A: Thresholds should come from the business requirement, not the prototype.
B: 87% is too low for any production system; it should be at least 95%.
C: Thresholds should be set after launch, once real production data is available.
D: Accuracy shouldn't have a threshold; only latency and cost should.
answer: A
why A: Correct. Success criteria are derived from what the business needs; prototype performance isn't a standard.
why B: The right number depends on the use case; the problem is where it came from.
why C: Thresholds must exist before you build, as acceptance criteria.
why D: Accuracy is a core metric.
lesson: Set thresholds from the business requirement, before building.
source: M2 Defining success criteria

## D4-002
objective: 4.1
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: A claims system must never leak one claimant's data into another's summary.
question: Which metric and grading method fits this security requirement best?
A: An LLM judge rating each summary for privacy appropriateness on a 1–10 scale.
B: Human review of a 1% sample of summaries each month by the privacy team.
C: The rate of user complaints about privacy, tracked on the dashboard.
D: A code check scanning each summary for identifiers from other claims.
answer: D
why A: Interpretive scoring is unnecessary for a deterministic property and can be inconsistent.
why B: Sampling is slow and may miss leaks entirely.
why C: Complaints are a lagging signal and depend on users noticing.
why D: Correct. Leakage across claimants can be checked deterministically by scanning for foreign identifiers.
lesson: If a property can be checked in code, check it in code.
source: M2 Exercise: define the evaluation framework (security)

## D4-003
objective: 4.1
difficulty: Medium
select: 2
scenario: An insurer's system must summarise claim narratives faithfully and must never auto-deny a claim.
question: Which TWO grading choices are most appropriate for these safety requirements? (Select 2)
A: A code-based binary check that no denial action was issued.
B: A code-based regex check for summary faithfulness.
C: An LLM judge, with a rubric, assessing whether the summary faithfully represents the source narrative.
D: Human review of every summary.
E: Track average summary length over time as a proxy for safety, flagging unusually short summaries.
answer: A,C
why A: Correct. "Never auto-deny" is binary and deterministic.
why B: Faithfulness needs interpretation that regex can't encode.
why C: Correct. Faithfulness is interpretive, which suits a calibrated judge.
why D: Reviewing everything doesn't scale and isn't needed as the primary method.
why E: Length isn't a safety measure.
lesson: Safety often needs both: code for binary rules, a judge for interpretive qualities.

## D4-004

scenario: A golden dataset contains only clean, typed claim forms. Production includes handwritten sections, missing fields and non-standard layouts.
question: What is the most important change to the evaluation?
A: Raise the accuracy threshold to compensate for the cleaner test data.
B: Add edge cases (missing fields, handwriting, odd layouts) as dataset categories.
C: Switch to a more capable judge model so scoring of messy inputs is more accurate.
D: Add more clean examples so the dataset is larger and more statistically reliable.
answer: B

## D4-005
objective: 4.2
difficulty: Easy
select: 1
scenario: A team must check that responses are valid JSON matching a schema, and separately that the tone is empathetic.
question: Which grading approach is correct?
A: Code-based checks for JSON/schema; a calibrated LLM judge for tone.
B: An LLM judge for both, so a single grading pipeline covers schema and tone.
C: Human review for both, since tone and schema both affect the customer.
D: Code-based checks for both, using a sentiment keyword list for tone.
answer: A
why A: Correct. Use the cheapest reliable method for each behaviour, moving up the grading ladder only as needed.
why B: A judge for schema validity costs more and is less reliable than code.
why C: Human review doesn't scale and isn't needed for either as the primary method.
why D: Code can't assess tone.
lesson: Code where possible, a judge where interpretation is needed, humans as the last resort.
source: M2 Grading ladder

## D4-006
objective: 4.2
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: An LLM judge scores outputs highly, but domain experts disagree with many verdicts. The judge uses the same model that generated the outputs and returns a bare 1–10 score.
question: Which changes make the judge trustworthy?
A: Widen the scale to 1–100 so the judge can express finer differences in quality.
B: Run the judge twice on each output and average the two scores to reduce noise.
C: Replace the judge with a larger model from the same family that produced the outputs.
D: Rubric, constrained verdicts with reasoning, human calibration, a different grader.
answer: D
why A: Finer scales amplify inconsistency.
why B: Averaging doesn't fix calibration or self-preference.
why C: The same family still risks self-preference, and calibration is still missing.
why D: Correct. Rubric, constrained verdicts, reasoning, calibration and a different grader model make judging rigorous.
lesson: An uncalibrated judge gives confident scores that may mean nothing.

## D4-007
objective: 4.2
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: A summarisation prompt was revised. The eval suite still passes, but field reports show multi-clause legal sentences being truncated.
question: What is the most likely cause?
A: The judge model is too strict and is rejecting correct summaries as failures.
B: The model version changed silently on the provider side without notice.
C: The eval set predates the prompt change and doesn't test the new behaviour.
D: Latency increased after the prompt change, causing responses to be cut off.
answer: C
why A: A strict judge would fail outputs, not pass them.
why B: There's no evidence of a version change; the pattern matches a stale eval.
why C: Correct. An out-of-date eval suite gives false confidence; keep it current with every change.
why D: Latency doesn't truncate sentence content in this way.
lesson: The riskiest regression moment is when evals exist but are out of date.

## D4-010

question: What was the primary flaw?
A: The test used the wrong model tier for the new prompt version.
B: The test should have measured latency, which is a more stable metric.
C: The prompts weren't cached, so the two arms ran under different conditions.
D: Too small a sample, and the metric wasn't chosen in advance.

## D4-012
objective: 4.3
difficulty: Hard
select: 1
scenario: A prompt change shows a statistically significant +3% task success rate, but cost per completion rises 30% and p95 latency rises 15%.
question: How should the architect interpret the result?
A: Ship it, because statistical significance confirms the improvement is real.
B: Discard it, because any cost increase outweighs a quality improvement.
C: Re-run the test until the cost increase is no longer statistically significant.
D: Weigh the practical gain against the cost and latency hit before shipping.
answer: D
why A: Significance shows the effect is unlikely to be chance, not that it's worth shipping.
why B: It might still be worth it depending on budget; the decision needs weighing, not a reflex.
why C: Re-running until you see what you want is p-hacking.
why D: Correct. Check practical size and secondary metrics before declaring a winner.
lesson: Significant isn't the same as worth shipping.

question: Which TWO first steps are most appropriate? (Select 2)

B: Immediately upgrade to the most capable model.

why D: Rewriting without a diagnosis may not address the cause.
why E: Retention doesn't diagnose anything.
