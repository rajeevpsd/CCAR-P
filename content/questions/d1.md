# Domain 1 – Solution Design & Architecture · Question bank (original practice items, not real exam questions)

## D1-001
objective: 1.1
difficulty: Easy
select: 1
scenario: A logistics partner asks for "AI that handles our carrier claims". Claims require reading carrier letters, checking contract rates in an existing rate engine, calculating refund amounts, and approving payouts above $5,000.
question: Which decomposition is most appropriate?
A: Claude reads letters, looks up contract rates from what it knows, calculates refunds, and approves all payouts.
B: Claude handles every step end to end, with a human spot-checking 5% of outputs each week.
C: The rate engine handles everything, and Claude only formats the final notification email to the carrier.
D: Claude extracts and drafts; the rate engine calculates; humans approve payouts over $5,000.
answer: D
why A: Over-assigning to Claude: rates are authoritative values, and arithmetic and approvals need deterministic systems or humans.
why B: Sampling doesn't fit high-value, hard-to-reverse payouts.
why C: Under-uses Claude for the language work (reading varied letters) it does well.
why D: Correct. Each part goes to the owner that fits: language to Claude, authoritative data to systems, high-stakes approval to humans.
lesson: Split work into Claude, existing systems and humans before choosing a pattern.
source: M1 Decomposition; M1 four properties

## D1-002
objective: 1.1
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: A sponsor watched a working demo of a contract-flagging assistant and asks the architect to confirm feasibility today. Volume, contract lengths and response-time needs haven't been discussed.
question: What should the architect do next?
A: Confirm feasibility now, because the working demo already proves the model has the capability.
B: Gather volume, input-size and latency needs, then give a verdict with boundary conditions.
C: Confirm feasibility, but add a 20% contingency to the timeline to cover unknown requirements.
D: Decline to give a verdict until a full production build has been tested at real volume.
answer: B
why A: Capability isn't feasibility; unknown constraints can invalidate the design.
why B: Correct. A feasibility verdict is only as sound as the constraints gathered before it, and it must name its boundary conditions.
why C: Contingency doesn't replace missing constraints.
why D: A verdict can be given at scoping time; it doesn't need a production build.
lesson: Collect volume, input size and latency before declaring feasibility.
source: M2 Sizing – "The scoping call that skipped the constraints"

## D1-003
objective: 1.1
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: A wealth-management firm wants Claude to answer client questions about current portfolio values and recent trades, plus general questions about the firm's investment philosophy.
question: Which property-driven design choice is most important?
A: Fine-tune the model on the investment philosophy so its answers sound consistently on-brand.
B: Enable extended thinking so the model recalls portfolio values and recent trades more accurately.
C: Fetch portfolio values and trades by authorised tool calls; retrieve the philosophy content.
D: Use the largest available context window and load every client's full portfolio history into it.

why D: Loading all histories inflates cost and exposes data; it's also stale.

## D1-004
objective: 1.1
difficulty: Hard
select: 1
scenario: A scoping assessment for a document summariser finds it works for documents up to 150 pages but degrades beyond that because of context limits on the chosen tier. 8% of production documents exceed 150 pages.
question: How should the feasibility verdict be stated?
A: Feasible as scoped, because 92% of production documents fall within the supported length.
B: Feasible with constraints: long documents need chunking or another tier; record it in the SOW.
C: Not feasible, because some production documents exceed what the chosen tier can handle.
D: Feasible as scoped, with a note to revisit long documents after launch if users report problems.
answer: B
why A: Ignoring a known boundary turns into a production failure for 8% of traffic.
why B: Correct. The verdict plus the constraint that makes it true, documented so both teams know what's in scope.
why C: The limitation can be compensated (chunking or tier), so "not feasible" is wrong.
why D: Deferring a known boundary means it goes undocumented, and an undocumented constraint fails in production.
lesson: "Feasible with constraints" means each constraint is documented and its failure mode named.
source: M2 Feasibility verdicts; M2 How to scope (boundary conditions)

## D1-005
objective: 1.2
difficulty: Easy
select: 1
scenario: An invoice-extraction pipeline sends every document through OCR → extraction → ERP posting. Wrong extractions on unusual layouts get posted to the ERP at the same rate as clean ones.
question: Which architectural element is missing?
A: A larger, more capable model for the extraction step so unusual layouts are read correctly.
B: Prompt caching for the extraction prompt so each document is processed faster and cheaper.
C: A nightly batch job that re-runs every document through the pipeline to catch errors.
D: Confidence scoring that routes low-confidence extractions to a human review queue first.
answer: D
why A: A bigger model may help averages but doesn't stop wrong edge cases reaching the ERP.
why B: Caching affects cost, not correctness.
why C: Re-running repeats the same flow without an exception gate.
why D: Correct. Document pipelines fail most often through a missing exception path; confidence-based routing to a human queue is the mitigation.
lesson: Every pipeline needs an exception path for low-confidence output.
source: M1 Reference architectures (document pipeline); M2 Failure modes

## D1-006
objective: 1.2
difficulty: Medium
select: 1
scenario: A knowledge assistant's architecture diagram ends at "response returned to user". After launch, quality erodes for weeks before anyone notices.
question: Which addition best completes the end-to-end design?
A: Outcome signals feeding evals and a governance table with triggers, owners and actions.
B: Higher-resolution latency dashboards with p50, p95 and p99 broken down by request type.
C: A second model that reviews and rewrites every response before it's returned to the user.
D: Monthly retraining of the model on recent user conversations so it keeps improving.
answer: A
why A: Correct. A feedback loop of outcome signals, evals, triggers and owners is part of the architecture, not an add-on.
why B: Latency dashboards don't detect quality erosion.
why C: Adds cost and latency without a detection mechanism.
why D: Retraining isn't a standard lever here, and it doesn't replace detection and review.
lesson: Design input → processing → output → feedback loop; the loop is what detects degradation.
source: M2 Observability (outcome logging); M4 Feedback loop

## D1-007
objective: 1.2
difficulty: Hard
select: 2
scenario: You are designing an end-to-end claims-summary system for adjusters. Inputs include claimant-submitted documents that may contain embedded instructions; outputs feed the claims system.
question: Which TWO design elements belong at the input and output boundaries respectively? (Select 2)
A: Input: screen submitted and retrieved content for injected instructions before it reaches the model, and inject identity server-side.
B: Input: allow claimants to specify which adjuster role they want the summary written for.
C: Output: validate against a schema and screen content before it is written to the claims system.
D: Output: return free-form text so adjusters have maximum flexibility.
E: Input: include every historical claim for the claimant in the prompt for context.
answer: A,C
why A: Correct. Indirect injection arrives through submitted and retrieved content; identity must come from the server.
why B: Self-asserted role selection is an identity-spoofing path.
why C: Correct. An output contract plus validation and screening protects the downstream system.
why D: Free-form output breaks downstream integration and is harder to validate.
why E: Over-loads context and over-exposes data; minimise to what the task needs.
lesson: Each stage of the end-to-end path has its own controls; input and output gates are different controls.
source: M3 Guardrails (indirect injection, output screening); M2 Identity, data minimisation

## D1-009

question: Which pattern fits best?

## D1-011

scenario: An engineering org wants Claude to investigate unfamiliar legacy codebases and propose fixes for reported bugs. The investigation path depends on what each file reveals. Fixes can be reviewed before merge.

A: An agent to investigate, then deterministic edit/test/review steps with review before merge.
B: A fixed prompt chain that reads the same five core files for every bug, then proposes a fix.

## D1-015

scenario: In a multi-agent due-diligence system, a long run fails when the orchestrator's process crashes after 70% of subagent work is complete, and everything restarts from zero.
question: Which design response best addresses this?

B: Merge all subagents into the orchestrator so there's only one process to keep alive.
