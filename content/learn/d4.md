# Episode 4 – Evaluation, Testing & Optimization
intro: Episode four, Evaluation, Testing and Optimization, is sixteen percent of the exam. Six parts: defining metrics, building eval sets, running real experiments, diagnosing failures, optimising cost and latency, and monitoring performance over time.

## Part 4.1 – Define evaluation metrics
minutes: 5
concepts: Behaviour to threshold; five dimensions; thresholds from the business; failure modes as categories; adversarial inputs
example: An insurer's framework: exact match on structured fields, p95 under target, cost per claim under the ceiling, no auto-denials, and a code scan for data leaking across claimants.
takeaway: If you can't write an eval for a behaviour, you can't verify it. Thresholds come from the business, not from the prototype.
narration:
"Summarise claims accurately" isn't a metric. To make it one, name the specific behaviour, like extracting the claimant, the policy number, the loss amount and the date. Then set a threshold, and that threshold comes from the business requirement, not from whatever your first prototype happened to score.

Next, list the failure modes: a made-up claim number, a missing date, a value taken from the wrong claim. Each failure mode becomes a category in your dataset. And include adversarial inputs, like handwriting, missing fields and strange layouts. A clean-only dataset predicts nothing about production.

The exam frames metrics in five dimensions: accuracy, latency, cost, safety and security. Most of these can be checked in code. Is the field right? Is p95 under target? Is cost under the ceiling? Was a denial issued? Did any other claimant's identifier appear in this summary? Those are all deterministic checks.

Some safety qualities, like whether a summary is faithful to its source, need interpretation, and that's where a calibrated judge comes in.
check: Where should an eval threshold come from? || A) The score the first prototype achieved || B) The business requirement || C) A competitor's published accuracy claim || D) Whatever production shows after launch || answer: B || why: Thresholds are acceptance criteria set before building.
check: Which method fits "never leak another claimant's data"? || A) A code-based scan for foreign identifiers || B) An LLM judge's privacy score out of ten || C) Monthly sampling by the privacy team || D) The rate of user complaints about privacy || answer: A || why: It's a deterministic property.
check: The dataset contains only clean forms. What do you add? || A) Many more clean forms for statistical power || B) A stricter accuracy threshold on clean data || C) A more capable judge model for scoring || D) Edge cases and adversarial categories || answer: D || why: Representative data makes scores meaningful.

## Part 4.2 – Design evaluation datasets and test frameworks using mixed methodologies
minutes: 7
concepts: Five-stage eval workflow; golden dataset; grading ladder; judge calibration with a different grader model; multi-turn evals; volume over perfection; keep evals current
example: A summarisation eval kept passing after a prompt change because its dataset predated the change, while production truncated multi-clause sentences.
takeaway: Use code where you can, a calibrated judge where you must, and humans as the last resort. Refresh the dataset every time behaviour changes.
narration:
An eval workflow runs in five stages. Define the task and its pass criteria. Build a golden dataset that's representative, with edge cases. Run automated checks. Score interpretive behaviours with a judge. Then read the results by category, not just as one number.

Choosing how to grade follows a ladder. First, code: schema checks, exact matches, length. It's fast, cheap and never drifts. Second, a model as judge, for things like tone or faithfulness. Make it rigorous with a detailed rubric, a small fixed set of verdicts, reasoning alongside each score, and calibration against human-labelled examples. Use a different model from the one being graded, to avoid self-preference. Third, and last, human review, for novel or high-stakes behaviour, and to calibrate the judge.

Two more rules. Favour volume over perfection: two thousand cheap, automatic cases catch more regressions than forty perfect ones, and they can run on every change. And conversations need multi-turn evals that score whole transcripts.

Finally, the riskiest moment is when an eval exists but is out of date. Every time the prompt or model changes, update the dataset to match.
check: A judge's scores disagree with experts, and it grades its own model's outputs. check: Users say the chatbot forgets earlier details. Which eval is missing? || A) Multi-turn || B) Latency || C) Single-turn adversarial || D) Cost || answer: A || why: Conversation behaviour needs conversation-level evals.
check: Forty perfect cases or two thousand automatic ones? || A) Forty || B) Neither || C) Wait for production || D) Two thousand || answer: D || why: Broad, cheap coverage catches more regressions.

## Part 4.3 – Conduct A/B testing and iterative improvements
minutes: 6
concepts: Falsifiable hypothesis; random sticky assignment; pre-specified primary metric; sample size; significance vs. practical size; secondary metrics; shadow testing
example: Fifty sessions per arm showed 68 vs. 62 percent and the team shipped. It settled at 61. The test was underpowered, the input mix differed, and the metric was picked after the fact.
takeaway: Write the hypothesis, the primary metric and the sample size before day one. Use shadow testing when exposing users isn't acceptable.
narration:
Experiments on LLM systems need more discipline than usual, because outputs are noisy.

Start with a hypothesis that's specific and falsifiable. Something like: "this instruction change will raise task success by at least five percent, without worsening p95 latency." Choose the primary metric before you start. If you pick it after seeing the results, you can always find one that moved.

Assign users or sessions randomly, and keep the assignment sticky. If one arm happens to get simpler queries, an apparent win may just reflect the input mix. Calculate your sample size from the effect you need to detect. LLM variance is high, so you usually need hundreds of samples, not fifty.

When results come in, ask two questions. Is the effect big enough to be worth maintaining a new version? And did anything else get worse, like cost or latency? Significant isn't the same as worth shipping.

When exposing users to an untested change isn't acceptable, as in many regulated settings, use shadow testing. The new version runs on copied live traffic, users still get the current version, and you score the shadow outputs offline.
check: A test assigned traffic by support queue. What's the problem? || A) The sample was too large || B) Non-random assignment || C) The wrong metric || D) No caching || answer: B || why: The groups aren't comparable.
check: Policy forbids exposing clinicians to untested outputs. Which approach? || A) Shadow testing || B) A live 50/50 split || C) A single-ward canary || D) Synthetic data only || answer: A || why: Shadow testing validates on real traffic without any exposure.
check: Plus three percent success, plus thirty percent cost, and significant. What now? || A) Ship it, since the result is significant || B) Discard it, since any cost increase is bad || C) Weigh practical size against the cost before deciding || D) Re-run until the cost increase isn't significant || answer: C || why: Significant isn't the same as worth it.

## Part 4.4 – Diagnose system issues
minutes: 6
concepts: Prompt failure; hallucination; model mismatch; orchestrator-workers failure; what changed; symptom to cause; Discernment
example: Extraction dropped on two document types right after an unevaluated move to a cheaper tier. That's model mismatch, fixed with per-type evals and routing.
takeaway: Classify the failure before fixing it. Grounding fixes hallucination, specification fixes prompt failure, and evals gate model choice.
narration:
When something goes wrong, classify it before you fix it, because each class has a different cure.

A prompt failure means the instruction was ambiguous, and the model filled the gap differently each time. Output formats vary run to run. The fix is in the prompt: state the format, the constraints, and what to do when a field is missing.

A hallucination is confident, fluent content that isn't grounded in the source, like citations to papers that don't exist. Stronger wording won't fix it. Grounding will: retrieval, tools, and checking that each cited thing actually exists.

Model mismatch means the tier is wrong for the task, often after a swap without an eval. Look for category-specific drops right after the change.

And in multi-agent systems, use a shared trace to tell a recoverable subagent failure from an orchestrator failure.

Always start by asking what changed: the model version, the prompt, the corpus, or the mix of inputs. A RAG system that turns wrong right after a document refresh, with the model and latency unchanged, points at retrieval and indexing first.

check: The assistant cites journal articles that don't exist. check: Quality slid over weeks with no code change. ## Part 4.5 – Optimize token usage, latency, and cost-performance trade-offs
minutes: 6
concepts: Per-step tiering; caching order and economics; progressive context; max_tokens; thinking only when measured; Batch API; distribution-based cost models
example: Running Opus on every step cost seven times the plan. Tiering the classifier to Haiku and summaries to Sonnet, with Opus only for final composition, cut cost seventy-one percent with no change in satisfaction.
takeaway: Cache what's constant, tier each step, trim the context, and batch work that can wait. Model the token distribution, not the average.
narration:
Optimising cost and latency means matching each lever to its driver.

If the same long prefix goes out on every call, cache it, with static content first. If the system runs one top-tier model everywhere, tier each step instead, proving each move with an eval. If context grows with every turn, switch to progressive context and compaction. If outputs run long, cap them and tighten the output contract. If thinking is switched on everywhere, keep it only where it measurably helps.

For bulk work that doesn't need an instant answer, like overnight reports, the Batch API trades waiting for a large discount. Check the current pricing, and confirm compliance coverage before sending regulated data through it.

Above all, build your cost model from the distribution of token usage, not the average. Most requests may be small, but a tail of long documents can account for most of the spend. One team found their long documents used eighty percent of the tokens, and an average-based estimate came in two to three times too low.
check: Three hundred thousand descriptions needed by morning, with no interactive users. What fits? || A) Synchronous, at maximum concurrency || B) Batch API || C) Top tier with thinking || D) Streaming || answer: B || why: Async work earns the batch discount.
check: The bill is far above a forecast built on average tokens. Why? || A) The cache TTL was too short for reuse || B) Output tokens were priced as input || C) The model tier was assumed, not measured || D) The heavy-tailed distribution was ignored || answer: D || why: Model the distribution, not the average.
check: Opus runs every step and cost is seven times plan. What's the approach? || A) Per-step evals and tiering || B) Switch every step to Haiku || C) Add caching and change nothing else || D) Turn extended thinking on || answer: A || why: Tier each step to the cheapest model that passes.

## Part 4.6 – Monitor system performance using logging and observability tools
minutes: 5
concepts: Trace fields; dashboards; threshold alerts vs. drift comparison; KPI translation layer; signal-to-owner mapping; per-request decomposition
example: A weekly eval re-run on sampled production traffic triggers an architect review after two consecutive weeks below threshold, even though error rate is flat.
takeaway: Monitor quality, not just uptime, and give every signal an owner and an action.
narration:
Monitoring answers four questions. What is the system doing? How well? When did it change? And why?

Your dashboards should show cost per request, p50 and p95 latency, task success based on downstream acceptance, and errors by type. Make them breakable per request and per segment, because aggregate views can look healthy while a small slice of traffic burns the budget.

Threshold alerts catch sharp problems: a cost spike well above the weekly average, or p95 crossing the SLA. Slow quality drift won't trip those. For drift, re-run evals on sampled production traffic, and compare output distributions over time.

The people paying for the deployment don't read traces. Build a translation layer at design time that maps technical metrics to business ones, like task success to first-contact resolution, or latency to handle time. Then when the business asks whether it's working, you answer with a live query instead of a reconstruction.

And finally, a signal nobody owns changes nothing. Every alert needs a named owner and a defined action.
