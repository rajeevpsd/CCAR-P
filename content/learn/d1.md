# Episode 1 – Solution Design & Architecture
intro: Welcome to episode one, Solution Design and Architecture. This domain is seventeen percent of the exam, so it's one of the big ones. Over six parts, we'll go from a vague business request to an architecture you can defend: who does what, which pattern fits, how multi-agent systems stay honest, and how to show the business why it's worth building.

## Part 1.1 – Translate business problems into Claude-based AI solutions
minutes: 7
concepts: Four properties of generative AI; Claude, systems and humans; scoping sequence; feasibility verdicts; boundary conditions
example: "Process insurance claims" becomes four capabilities: field extraction by Claude with a verifier, coverage lookup from the policy database, routing by the rules engine, and a notification drafted by Claude and reviewed by the adjuster.
takeaway: Capability is not feasibility. Gather volume, input size and latency, then give a verdict with its constraints.
narration:
Let's start with the move that comes before any architecture. A partner rarely hands you a clean requirement. They say something like, "we want AI to handle our claims." Your first job is to break that into separate capabilities, and give each one an owner.

There are three owners. Claude takes the language work: reading, summarising, drafting, planning. Existing systems keep anything the partner has already paid to make reliable, like the policy database or the rules engine. Humans keep the judgement calls, the approvals and the exceptions.

To decide where Claude fits, use the four properties. Next-token prediction means Claude is strong on patterns but can be fluently wrong on specifics like dates and amounts. Knowledge means rare or changing facts should come from an external source of truth. Working memory means the context window is a hard edge. And steerability means concrete instructions work, while abstract ones drift.

Finally, never confirm feasibility just because a demo worked. Ask about volume, input size and latency first. Then give one of three verdicts: feasible as scoped, feasible with constraints, or not feasible. And write the constraints into the statement of work.

check: Which owner should calculate refund amounts against contract rates? || A) Claude, using the rates it learned in training || B) A human analyst, recalculating every claim || C) The existing rate engine || D) A subagent || answer: C || why: Authoritative values and arithmetic belong to deterministic systems.
check: A design works up to 150 pages, and 8% of documents are longer. What is the verdict? || A) Feasible as scoped, since 92% of documents fit || B) Not feasible || C) Feasible with constraints, documented || D) Feasible, with long documents revisited after launch || answer: C || why: State the verdict plus the boundary condition that makes it true.

## Part 1.2 – Design end-to-end architectures
minutes: 6
concepts: Input, processing, output, feedback loop; exception paths; outcome signals; governance triggers
example: Invoice processing: OCR and a server-side tenant ID going in, schema extraction with an evaluator pass, validated JSON with low-confidence fields routed to a human queue, and reviewer corrections feeding the golden dataset.
takeaway: A diagram that ends at "response to user" is missing the feedback loop. The loop is how a system notices it's degrading.
narration:
An end-to-end architecture has four stages, and each stage needs its own controls.

At the input, identity comes from your server, not from the user's message. Data is cut down to what the task actually needs. And anything coming in, including retrieved documents, is screened.

Processing is where you choose the pattern and the reference architecture, the model tier and the context strategy.

At the output, there's a contract: a schema, a validation step, output screening, and a confidence score that decides whether a person needs to look first.

And then the stage most diagrams forget: the feedback loop. You log outcomes, like whether the downstream system accepted the result, or a reviewer corrected it. Those signals feed your evals and a governance table with triggers and owners. Without that loop, a system can quietly drift for weeks and nobody notices.

One more thing. Every pipeline needs an exception path. If messy documents travel the same road as clean ones, wrong outputs flow downstream at the same rate as right ones.
check: Wrong extractions post to the ERP as often as right ones. What's missing? || A) A larger, more capable extraction model || B) Prompt caching || C) A confidence-based exception path || D) A nightly re-run of every document batch || answer: C || why: Low-confidence outputs need their own route to human review.
check: Quality eroded for weeks before anyone noticed. Which stage was missing? || A) Input screening for injected content || B) The feedback loop || C) Model selection for each pipeline step || D) Output schema || answer: B || why: Outcome signals and triggers are what detect degradation.
check: Where does verified user identity come from? || A) The user's own message, as they state it || B) A role field in the system prompt template || C) The server, from authentication || D) The model || answer: C || why: Self-asserted identity can be faked.

## Part 1.3 – Select appropriate architectural patterns
minutes: 8
concepts: Augmented LLM; workflow vs. agent; five workflow sub-patterns; five decision factors; prompting before fine-tuning
example: A disbursement agent's traces showed only four paths. A router with four chains gave the auditor a discrete approval step.
takeaway: If you can write the steps down, write them in code. Use an agent only when the path really can't be listed in advance.
narration:
There are three shapes Claude's involvement can take.

An augmented LLM is a single call. It may use tools or retrieval, but it does one bounded job, and the control flow never branches on what the model decides.

A workflow is a set of steps orchestrated in your own code. You can log it, test it, and point to exactly which step did what.

An agent is different. You give the model a goal and tools, and the model decides its own sequence of steps. The control flow lives inside the model.

Workflows come in five common shapes. Prompt chaining, with checks between steps. Routing, when the categories are clear. Parallelization, either splitting into sections or voting. Orchestrator-workers, when the subtasks can't be known until runtime. And evaluator-optimizer, when you can generate, check against criteria, and refine.

To choose, walk five factors in order: predictability, error cost, observability, latency and cost. The tightest constraint picks the pattern.

And when prompting feels unreliable, don't jump to fine-tuning. Improve the prompt first, then add tools or retrieval, then try a stronger pattern. Fine-tuning comes last, if at all.
check: Five known email categories with accurate classification. Which pattern fits? || A) Autonomous agent || B) Routing workflow || C) Multi-agent system || D) Fine-tuned model per category || answer: B || why: Distinct categories with accurate classification is the routing case.
check: Which situation justifies an agent? || A) The steps are known but there are many of them || B) The path depends on what each step uncovers || C) The auditor needs to see a named approval step || D) Latency is very tight || answer: B || why: Agents fit work where the path can't be listed in advance.
check: Prompting seems unreliable for an extraction task. What comes next? || A) Fine-tune a model on the extraction examples || B) Switch to an agent || C) Improve the prompt, then tools, then a stronger pattern || D) Use the biggest model and skip the eval set || answer: C || why: Fine-tuning is the last step in the progression.

## Part 1.4 – Design multi-agent systems and orchestration strategies
minutes: 7
concepts: Orchestrator and subagents; fan-out; asymmetric error recovery; coverage check; conflict resolution; HITL gates
example: A 50-section contract review reported "48 reviewed, 3 flagged" because two subagents silently returned nothing. A coverage check would have flagged the gap.
takeaway: Reconcile coverage, protect orchestrator state, and use the fewest agents that meet the requirement.
narration:
Some work is too large or too varied for one agent to hold in a single context. That's where multi-agent systems come in.

The orchestrator owns the goal. It breaks the work apart, delegates and synthesises, but it doesn't do the sub-tasks itself. Subagents each own one scoped piece, in their own clean context.

The most common shape is fan-out: split a big item into independent units, run one subagent per unit in parallel, then combine the results.

Here's the key design insight. Failure is asymmetric. If a subagent fails, that's usually recoverable: retry it, re-route it, or flag the gap. If the orchestrator fails, the whole run can be lost. So checkpoint the orchestrator's state, and make subagent work safe to retry.

The most dangerous failure is a silent one. The summary looks complete, but some units never came back. The fix is a coverage check: results returned must equal units dispatched.

Also design how conflicts get resolved, give every agent the same trace ID, and gate any irreversible action behind a person. Anthropic's own research system found multi-agent setups use roughly fifteen times the tokens of chat, so use them only when the work truly needs them.
check: Two subagents timed out and the summary still looked complete. What's the fix? || A) A more capable model for the orchestrator || B) Longer timeouts || C) A coverage check at synthesis || D) Running every section sequentially instead || answer: C || why: Results returned must equal units dispatched.
check: Which failure is usually unrecoverable? || A) A subagent returning a malformed result || B) Two subagents returning conflicting results || C) The orchestrator losing its state || D) A subagent timeout || answer: C || why: The orchestrator holds the goal and the synthesis, so protect it.
check: When is multi-agent a poor fit? || A) The work is far larger than one context || B) The units are independent || C) The steps depend heavily on each other || D) Breadth-first research across many sources || answer: C || why: Heavy dependencies and shared context don't suit multi-agent.

## Part 1.5 – Apply decomposition techniques for complex problem solving
minutes: 5
concepts: Delegation map; reversibility, stakes, accountability; capability decomposition; chains with gates; only independent units in parallel
example: Law-firm contract review: iManage fetches documents, Claude compares clauses against the playbook section by section in parallel, and the senior associate signs off.
takeaway: Decompose by owner first, then by step. Gate between steps, and parallelise only what is truly independent.
narration:
Decomposition happens at three levels.

The first is ownership: Claude, systems or humans. You justify each assignment with three questions. Can a wrong call be undone? What does a wrong call cost? And who has to answer for it? That's reversibility, stakes and accountability. The result is a delegation map, where some work is Claude's, some stays human, and some is collaborative, with Claude drafting and a person deciding.

The second level is capabilities. Turn the business goal into a list of named things the system must do, so each one can get the right owner.

The third level is the work itself. Chain steps with checks between them. Fan out independent units. And when an input is bigger than the context window, decompose the input itself, by section, with a synthesis step and a coverage check at the end.

One trap to avoid: only parallelise units that are truly independent. If the executive summary depends on three sections, those sections can run in parallel, but the summary has to wait for them.
check: Which three criteria justify a delegation decision? || A) Speed, cost and the size of the model || B) Reversibility, stakes, accountability || C) Volume, latency and token consumption || D) Accuracy, fluency, tone || answer: B || why: These decide whether Claude should own a decision.
check: Three independent sections plus a summary that depends on them. What's the plan? || A) Run all four parts in parallel at once || B) One pass || C) Sections in parallel, then a gated summary || D) Chain all four parts with no checks between || answer: C || why: Parallelise only the independent units.
check: The input is larger than the context window. Which approach fits? || A) Truncate the input to what fits in the window || B) Summarise the whole input in one model call first || C) Split by section, extract, then synthesise with a coverage check || D) Use the full input and accept any truncation || answer: C || why: Decompose the input when it exceeds working memory.

## Part 1.6 – Align solutions to business value pillars
minutes: 6
concepts: Five pillars; ROI mapping in four steps; measured baseline; net of review effort; payback with sensitivity; outcome document
example: Claims triage drops from 40 to 15 analyst minutes, because review is still required. Net value is the hours saved minus the monthly run cost from sizing, with payback shown at lower volume too.
takeaway: Technical metrics show the system runs. A before-and-after business metric shows it's worth expanding.
narration:
A feasible design isn't automatically a worthwhile one. Here's how to make the business case.

There are five value pillars. Efficiency means the same work, faster or cheaper. Transformation means work that wasn't possible before, like reviewing every case instead of a small sample. Productivity means more output from the same people. Cost means the run cost of the solution itself. And performance SLAs are the service levels the deployment has to hold.

Then build the ROI in four steps. First, measure today's baseline in a unit the business already tracks, using their own data, not intuition. Second, project the new state in the same unit, and remember: if a person still reviews some outputs, that time is still a cost. Third, subtract the monthly run cost, taken from your sizing distribution, not an average. Fourth, give a payback period, and show how it moves if volume or gains come in lower.

When you close the engagement, the outcome document needs the business metric before and after, the control that makes that comparison auditable, and an owner who keeps measuring it.
check: A deployment lets compliance review every communication instead of five percent. Which pillar? || A) Efficiency || B) Transformation || C) Cost || D) Performance SLA || answer: B || why: Work that wasn't feasible before is transformation.
check: The business case assumes zero analyst hours, but twenty percent of items go to review. What's the flaw? || A) Nothing; the projection is reasonable || B) It overstates value by ignoring review effort || C) It should count tokens saved rather than hours || D) Review time belongs in the one-off build cost || answer: B || why: Net human review effort out of the projected gain.
