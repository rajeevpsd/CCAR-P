# Episode 5 – Governance, Safety & Risk Management
intro: Episode five, Governance, Safety and Risk Management, is fourteen percent of the exam. Five parts: building guardrails, naming risks, routing decisions to humans, turning regulations into evidenced controls, and designing for fairness and transparency.

## Part 5.1 – Implement guardrails and safety controls
minutes: 8
concepts: Training-time alignment vs. inference-time control; four layers; input screening, output screening, tool authorization; fail closed; indirect injection; refusal handling; Skill supply chain
example: A refund agent's only guardrail was an output filter, which checked the text after the refund had already run. Deterministic authorization before the tool would have stopped it.
takeaway: Claude can't enforce a rule it was never given. Gate inputs, outputs and actions, and make each control fail closed.
narration:
Claude arrives with broad safety behaviour built in. Anthropic trains it against a constitution that prioritises being broadly safe, ethical, compliant and genuinely helpful. That reduces broad harm. But training has never seen your partner's data rules or domain policy. If you assume it covers them, the rule goes unenforced everywhere.

So think in four layers. Trained behaviour, owned by Anthropic. The system prompt, which steers but doesn't enforce. Runtime screening. And authorization. You own the last three.

On the request path, there are three control points. Input screening, before the model call. Output screening, before the response reaches the user. And tool-call authorization, before any action with side effects. Authorization should be deterministic: an allowlist plus identity and scope. One filter at the end doesn't cover the other two points.

Decide what each control does when it errors. A guardrail that passes traffic when it fails gives the look of protection with none of the function. Fail closed.

Screen retrieved content and tool outputs as well, because that's where indirect injection arrives. And before installing third-party Skills, audit them, run them sandboxed with least privilege, and take them only from trusted sources.
check: A domain policy was assumed to be covered by Claude's training. What's the result? || A) It's enforced by the constitution || B) It isn't enforced in any layer || C) Output screening covers it || D) It works on larger tiers || answer: B || why: Domain rules must live in a layer you build.
check: The screening service times out and traffic passes. What should it do instead? || A) Fail open to keep traffic flowing || B) Retry the check indefinitely || C) Fail closed and log || D) Switch off screening at peak hours || answer: C || why: A guardrail that fails open isn't protecting anything.
check: Injected instructions arrive inside retrieved documents. What's the control? || A) Screen retrieved content before it enters context || B) Strengthen the user-input classifier further || C) Add a system-prompt rule to ignore such text || D) Lower top-k so fewer documents are retrieved || answer: A || why: Indirect injection bypasses user-input screening.

## Part 5.2 – Identify risks, limitations, and failure modes of LLM systems
minutes: 6
concepts: Direct and indirect injection; token-budget exhaustion; tool abuse; data exposure; non-determinism; walking the request and data paths; written risk assessment
example: A RAG and tools agent: screen uploaded PDFs for injection, cap input size and budgets, put an allowlist and recipient rule on send_email, and redact PII before logging.
takeaway: Walk every entry point, name the risk category, and record each one with a control, an owner and evidence.
narration:
Most LLM risks fall into five categories. Direct prompt injection: a user tries to override the instructions. Indirect prompt injection: malicious instructions arrive through retrieved content or tool output. Token-budget exhaustion: huge or padded inputs that truncate work or inflate cost. Tool abuse: the model is induced to call a side-effecting tool outside policy. And data exposure: sensitive fields ending up in the context window or the logs.

On top of these sit the model's own limitations. It can be confidently wrong. Its knowledge can be stale. Its working memory has a hard edge. And it isn't deterministic. A demo that gives the same answer five times proves nothing about the sixth.

Failure modes also depend on the architecture. Agents run away on tool use and context. RAG drifts as the corpus changes. Document pipelines lack exception paths. Multi-agent systems drop units silently.

To assess a system, walk the request and data paths: user input, retrieved content, tool outputs, model output, and logs. At each point, ask what an attacker could do and what stops them. Then write it down. A written risk assessment, with a control, an owner and evidence for every risk, is what the security reviewer signs.
check: Hidden instructions sit on a web page the agent reads. Which category? || A) Direct prompt injection || B) Data residency violation || C) Token-budget exhaustion || D) Indirect injection || answer: D || why: The payload arrives through content the agent retrieves.
check: Users paste giant padded logs, causing truncation and cost spikes. What's the control? || A) Input limits, max_tokens and per-turn budgets || B) Redact sensitive values from the logs || C) Add retrieval to ground the answers || D) Put authorization on the log tools || answer: A || why: Bound the budget to prevent exhaustion.
check: What does a security reviewer sign off on? || A) The architecture diagram, annotated with data flows || B) A written risk assessment with controls, owners and evidence || C) The latest eval scores for quality and safety || D) A list of known limitations from public model docs || answer: B || why: The assessment is a deliverable.

## Part 5.3 – Apply human-in-the-loop validation strategies
minutes: 6
concepts: Reversibility, cost and calibrated confidence; pre-action, post-action, sampled; reviewer view; consent fatigue; plan-level review; Diligence
example: A near-threshold benefits applicant with incomplete documents and a recommended deny goes to pre-action caseworker review, with the inputs and the flag reason shown.
takeaway: If it can't be undone and the system isn't sure, a person decides first. Route by stakes, not by volume.
narration:
Think of human review as a budget. Reviewer attention is limited, so spend it where it matters most.

Two variables set the stakes: how reversible the decision is, and what a wrong decision would cost. A third, confidence, estimates how likely this particular output is to be wrong. But confidence only helps if it's calibrated against real outcomes, because a model can be confidently wrong.

The rule: route to a person when confidence is low and the decision is irreversible or expensive. Let confident, reversible, cheap decisions through.

Then choose where the person sits. Pre-action approval means nothing irreversible happens unreviewed, but it adds latency. Post-action audit keeps throughput high, but only suits reversible decisions. Sampled review monitors quality without guarding each individual case.

What the reviewer sees matters as much as what they're sent. Show the inputs, the output, and the reason the item was flagged.

And beware consent fatigue. If people are asked to approve dozens of times in a row, they stop reading. For agents, move review up to the plan and to exceptions, and let permissions bound everything else.
check: A wrong denial would remove benefits, and confidence is low. Where does review go? || A) Post-action audit || B) Five percent sampling || C) No review above 0.6 || D) Pre-action approval || answer: D || why: Irreversible, high-cost and uncertain means a human goes first.
check: Reviewers see four hundred items a day with only an approve button. What two fixes? || A) Hire more reviewers and keep routing everything || B) Remove human review, since it's just approvals || C) Route by stakes, and show inputs and the flag reason || D) Give each reviewer a daily approval quota || answer: C || why: Fix both the volume and the missing context.
check: What must be true before routing on a confidence score? || A) It's calibrated against outcomes || B) It's set above 0.95 for every decision || C) The model explains the score in its output || D) It's derived from the length of the response || answer: A || why: Uncalibrated confidence is unreliable.

## Part 5.4 – Ensure compliance with regulations
minutes: 8
concepts: Constraints eliminate options first; BAA per configuration; region pinning; FedRAMP routes; data minimisation; control register; evidence artifacts; training use vs. retention
example: A logging change started writing metadata to a second region. No one owned the residency control, so it surfaced only at audit. An owner, a data-flow record and scheduled checks would have caught it.
takeaway: A compliant route is a prerequisite, not proof. Each obligation needs a control, an owner and living evidence.
narration:
Compliance works in two steps.

First, constraints remove options before anything else is decided. Attorney-client privilege rules out consumer tools and points to the API behind the firm's own gateway, with single sign-on and full logging. HIPAA requires a business associate agreement covering the specific configuration and features in use, and beta features are generally excluded. A requirement to keep data in the EU points, per the modules, to a cloud route pinned to an EU region. Government workloads need a route authorised at the required FedRAMP level. These specifics change, so always verify the current status.

Inside whatever route survives, minimise data. If the language task doesn't need a field, like a social security number, it shouldn't be in the context window, where it can land in application logs. Redact it on the server before the call.

Second, turn each obligation into a control register: the technical control, an accountable owner, and an evidence artifact a reviewer will accept, like a signed agreement, a configuration screen, or a live log query. A design document isn't evidence.

Revalidate on a schedule, because configurations drift. And don't confuse training use with retention. Data excluded from training may still be retained for other purposes.
check: A healthcare partner has a BAA and wants to use a new beta feature. What do you verify? || A) Nothing more, since a BAA is already signed || B) That this configuration and feature are BAA-eligible || C) Only that data is encrypted in transit || D) Only that the feature is offered in-region || answer: B || why: Coverage is per configuration and feature.
check: Social security numbers land in app logs although the task doesn't need them. What's the fix? || A) Encrypt the application logs at rest || B) Shorten log retention to seven days || C) Tell the model not to repeat identifiers || D) Redact on the server before the call || answer: D || why: Data minimisation keeps unneeded fields out of context.
check: What does an auditor accept as proof that a control is operating? || A) A design document describing the control || B) An evidence artifact such as a configuration or a log query || C) The vendor's compliance marketing pages || D) An architecture diagram of the compliant route || answer: B || why: Proof means showing the control is live.

## Part 5.5 – Address ethical AI considerations
minutes: 6
concepts: Four fairness injection points; segment-level metrics; explanations for users, regulators and the team; decision logging; neutral prompts and balanced examples
example: A team assumed fairness was the model vendor's job. The skew came from their retrieval corpus, and they had too little logging to prove it.
takeaway: Fairness enters at points you control. Instrument them, and log decisions well enough to explain any one of them.
narration:
It's tempting to treat fairness as the model provider's problem. They trained the model and published bias evaluations, after all. But once you pair that model with your own data and logic, unequal outcomes can come in through four doors you control.

The retrieval corpus may over- or under-represent some groups. The prompt's framing may assume an answer. Few-shot examples may all show one kind of case. And downstream routing may send some groups down different paths.

So instrument each of those points, and measure outcomes by segment. An overall metric can look fine while harm concentrates in one subgroup.

Transparency means being able to explain any decision to three different audiences. An affected person needs a reason they can act on. A regulator needs evidence that comparable cases were treated consistently, and the ability to reconstruct a specific decision. Your team needs the full trace to debug it.

All three depend on decision logging: the inputs, the retrieved context, the output and the routing, kept so one decision can be replayed. Remember that the log is sensitive data itself, so minimise it, limit retention, and control access.
check: The model passed the vendor's bias evals, but outcomes are still unequal. Where do you look? || A) Only the vendor's original training data || B) Nowhere, since the vendor's evals settle it || C) System latency for different applicant groups || D) Corpus, prompt framing, examples and routing || answer: D || why: These are the injection points you control.
check: A regulator asks you to reconstruct one adverse decision. What do you need? || A) Decision-level logs || B) An aggregate fairness metric || C) The system prompt || D) Satisfaction scores || answer: A || why: Reconstruction needs per-decision records.
check: Every few-shot example shows approvals from one region. What's the fix? || A) Add more examples from the same region || B) Phrase neutrally and balance the examples || C) Remove every example and go zero-shot || D) Switch to a larger, less biased model || answer: B || why: Unbalanced examples build bias into the prompt.
