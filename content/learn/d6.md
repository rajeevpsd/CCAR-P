# Episode 6 – Stakeholder Communication & Lifecycle Management
intro: Episode six covers stakeholder communication and lifecycle management, fourteen percent of the exam. Five parts: structured discovery, communicating trade-offs, feedback loops and SLAs, documentation that survives a handoff, and moving through the lifecycle. Partner-track demo and sales content is optional and not tested.

## Part 6.1 – Conduct structured discovery and requirement gathering
minutes: 6
concepts: Listen, translate, write down; preference vs. constraint; must do, must not do, must cost, must prove; translation table; requirement vs. assumption
example: "We want it to feel seamless" became a p95 latency target, no re-entering upstream data, exceptions routed quietly to a reviewer, and failures that never expose internals.
takeaway: A preference tells you to keep asking. Finish all four question buckets before you sketch anything.
narration:
Discovery isn't a casual conversation. It's a structured elicitation with three steps: listen, translate, and write it down.

Translation is the core skill. Stakeholders speak in preferences: seamless, fast, simple. Designs are built against constraints. When you hear a preference, ask what would break it. What should the user never notice? What has to stay true when something fails? The answers become constraints you can test, like a latency target, a handoff rule, or a safe failure path.

Work through four buckets. What the system must do, as business outcomes. What it must not do: prohibited actions, and cases that must go to a person. What it must cost: budgets, latency, volume. And what it must prove: the evidence and audit obligations. Stakeholders rarely volunteer the last two, so ask for them directly.

Record each item as a row in a translation table: what was said, the constraint it implies, the architectural decision it forces, and any assumption still to confirm. Label assumptions clearly.

And watch for the classic trap. When an architect starts sketching mid-call, a confident design makes everyone assume the questions have been answered. Phrases like "it's just a quick review" often hide a mandatory approval step.
check: A stakeholder says, "make it seamless". What's the next move? || A) Write "seamless" down as a requirement || B) Propose streaming || C) Ask what would make it not seamless || D) Ask them to define it later || answer: C || why: Translate the preference into testable constraints.
check: Which question buckets are stakeholders least likely to volunteer? || A) Must not do, and must prove || B) Must do, and must cost || C) UI colours and project naming || D) Timelines and team staffing || answer: A || why: Boundaries and evidence obligations must be asked for.
check: How should an unconfirmed item be recorded? || A) As a confirmed requirement in the spec || B) As a fact, since it seems obvious || C) Left out until someone raises it again || D) As a labelled assumption with an owner || answer: D || why: Unsourced assumptions are the most dangerous kind.

## Part 6.2 – Communicate architectural decisions and trade-offs
minutes: 6
concepts: Gain, give up, reversal cost, compliance posture; decision package; translating to business units; Description for stakeholders; honest limits
example: A CTO approved "four cents a call" without hearing that, at production volume, it meant a five-figure monthly bill, or what unwinding the choice would cost. "I approved a direction, not a number."
takeaway: Present every trade-off with its gain, what it gives up, and what reversing it would cost, all in business terms.
narration:
Your job in a decision meeting isn't to deliver a verdict. It's to make an informed decision possible.

Every option you present needs three elements. What it gains. What it gives up. And what reversing it would cost once the system has been built around it. In regulated settings, add a fourth: what it does to the compliance posture.

The reversal cost is the element people forget, and it's the one that changes the meeting. It turns "which is technically better?" into "which is the better business choice?"

Translate the numbers too. A per-call cost means little until you multiply it by real production volume and show the monthly total.

Present the decision as a package: the options you considered, the criteria, your recommendation, and the risks that remain. The stakeholder will have to defend this choice to their own leadership, so give them that justification.

Lead with the business outcome, not the architecture. And state limits honestly. A security stakeholder trusts a system whose limits are spelled out far more than one whose limits they discover later.
check: Which trade-off element is most often missing? || A) The gain || B) The give-up || C) The reversal cost || D) An architecture diagram || answer: C || why: It's what changes the decision.
check: A CFO asks, "what happens if we choose wrong?" What do you do? || A) Explain it again with more technical detail || B) Defer the decision to the engineering team || C) Recommend the cheapest option to end the debate || D) Translate into business consequences, including reversal cost || answer: D || why: Stakeholders need consequences, not jargon.
check: A sceptical security stakeholder is in the room. Which approach works best? || A) State limits and their controls honestly || B) Avoid mentioning limitations at all || C) Provide a sixty-page technical appendix || D) Promise the limits will be fixed next release || answer: A || why: Clearly stated limits build trust.

## Part 6.3 – Manage stakeholder feedback loops and expectation alignment, including SLAs
minutes: 6
concepts: Signals, Triage, Decide, Act, Review; SLA as measure, breach and consequence; sourced thresholds; cost forecast; scheduled regulated checkpoints; iterate vs. re-architect
example: An eval score drifted for seven weeks with no alert, because no rule tied slow drift to a trigger and an owner. A quarterly review finally caught it.
takeaway: Monitoring isn't a feedback loop. Every signal needs a trigger, an owner and an action, and some reviews run on a calendar.
narration:
Observability tells you what the system is doing. A feedback loop decides what to do about it. It runs in five steps: signals, triage, decide, act, review.

Build it as a governance table before launch. Each row maps a signal, like eval score, p95 latency, cost per interaction, or data residency, to a review trigger, an owner and an action. Include slow drifts, not just hard failures. A score that sinks a little each week never trips an error alert.

An SLA names three things: what's measured, what counts as a breach, and what happens when one occurs. And every threshold should trace to something real. Latency to the user-experience expectation from discovery. Availability to how critical the business process is. Quality to your eval acceptance criteria.

Cost is the expectation that breaks most often after launch. Production volume can be ten to a hundred times the pilot. Give a forecast, and a spend-control plan, before the first invoice.

In regulated work, some reviews run on a schedule whether or not anything looks wrong, like periodic output audits or residency checks. Wire them in before launch.

What was missing? || A) More dashboards showing the same score || B) A higher error-rate alert threshold || C) A governance rule tying drift to a trigger and owner || D) A larger eval dataset per category || answer: C || why: Signals need triggers and owners.
check: Where should a latency SLA threshold come from? || A) The fastest result seen in load testing || B) The user-experience expectation from discovery || C) A competitor's published response times || D) The model's documented maximum speed || answer: B || why: Thresholds trace to real sources.

## Part 6.4 – Document architectures and provide implementation guidance
minutes: 6
concepts: Three readers; decisions plus rejected alternatives; evidence for reviewers; dated decisions and labelled assumptions; safe-change test; outcome document; runbooks; entry-point-responsibility map
example: A successor switched context strategies to fix performance and broke data residency, because the diagram showed what the system was but not why.
takeaway: Record why, and why not. The test is whether someone who wasn't in the room can make a safe change.
narration:
Documentation is what keeps a design working after you've left. It serves three readers.

The engineer who inherits the system needs the decisions, plus the alternatives you rejected and why. Without those, they can't tell which choices are load-bearing. They may reverse the right decision for an understandable wrong reason.

The compliance reviewer needs each obligation mapped to its control, its owner, and current evidence. Statements that a control exists aren't enough.

And the architect who returns months later, maybe you, needs dated decisions, assumptions clearly labelled as assumptions, and open items with owners.

The completeness test is practical. Could a competent architect who wasn't in any of the design sessions read this and make a safe change? If not, it isn't done.

Two more artifacts belong here. For deployments spanning several entry points, an entry-point-responsibility map says which route owns which task and why, so tasks don't quietly drift onto the wrong route. And the outcome document records the business metric before and after, the auditable control, and the measurement owner.
check: A successor broke residency by changing a load-bearing decision. What was missing? || A) A more detailed architecture diagram || B) More code comments in the repository || C) A list of team members and their roles || D) The rationale and rejected alternatives || answer: D || why: A diagram shows what, not why.
check: What's the completeness test for documentation? || A) Whether it runs to over fifty pages || B) Whether the sponsor has signed it off || C) Whether an outsider can make a safe change || D) Whether every log line is included in it || answer: C || why: This is the safe-change test.
check: Regulated data drifted onto the wrong route over time. Which artifact prevents this? || A) An entry-point-responsibility map || B) A larger eval set with regulated cases || C) Moving every task onto a single route || D) More logging on the direct-API path || answer: A || why: It makes route boundaries explicit.

## Part 6.5 – Support lifecycle phases
minutes: 5
concepts: Discovery, design, handoff, monitoring, iteration; POC-to-production gap; reliability controls by layer; version pinning; iterate vs. re-architect
example: A 529 overloaded error at peak took down a workflow that had no retries, no circuit breaker and no fallback. The demo had never shown any of these needs.
takeaway: A POC proves capability, nothing more. Know which phase you're in, and when monitoring should send you back to design.
narration:
Every deployment moves through five phases: discovery, design, handoff, monitoring and iteration.

Discovery produces the translation table. Design produces the pattern, the sizing, the eval suite written before code, the feasibility verdict, the trade-off package and the control register. Handoff produces the decision log, the runbooks and the responsibility map. Monitoring runs the observability stack and the governance table. And iteration runs eval-gated changes and experiments.

The riskiest transition is from proof of concept to production. A POC runs at low volume, on clean inputs, with patient users. It proves the system can do the task. It tells you nothing about cost at scale, p95 latency under load, reliability, or how it fails on inputs nobody tested.

So before production, add the reliability controls, each at its own layer. Retries with exponential backoff close to the API call. Circuit breakers at the service boundary. Fallback chains in the orchestration layer. Pin model versions, and keep an update runbook.

And know when iteration isn't enough. If the constraints themselves change, like triple the volume or a new residency rule, go back to design.
check: A POC worked perfectly at thirty requests a day. What does it prove? || A) Production readiness || B) Capability only || C) Cost at scale || D) p95 under load || answer: B || why: Cost, latency, reliability and failure modes still need design.
check: Where do retries with exponential backoff belong? || A) Near the API call || B) Inside the prompt || C) Only in orchestration || D) At the user interface || answer: A || why: Each reliability control works at its own layer.
check: Volume tripled, and a residency rule arrived. What's the lifecycle move? || A) Keep tuning prompts || B) Hand off as is || C) Drop the SLA || D) Re-architect || answer: D || why: Changed constraints send you back to design.
