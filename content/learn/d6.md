# Episode 6 – Stakeholder Management & Lifecycle
intro: Episode six covers stakeholder management and the deployment lifecycle, fourteen percent of the exam. Five parts: running structured discovery, communicating trade-offs so a stakeholder can actually decide, managing feedback loops and SLAs, documenting for handoff and audit, and supporting each phase of the lifecycle from discovery through iteration.

## Part 6.1 – Conduct structured discovery and requirement gathering
minutes: 10
concepts: Discovery as structured elicitation; listen, translate, write down; preferences hide constraints; four question categories (must do, must not do, must cost, must prove); testable, bounded constraints; translation table; requirement vs. assumption; design sketch too early
example: A hospital network wanted Claude to draft clinical notes from dictation. The architect proposed an augmented pattern and a prototype within minutes. Two weeks later compliance found three missed constraints: mandatory clinician authorisation, PHI in dictation, and different retention rules across two states.
decision: Finish all four question categories before proposing anything, translate every preference word into a testable constraint, and record each item as a row with its assumption labelled.
takeaway: A stated preference is a signal to ask more questions, not a requirement. A confident sketch ends the questions discovery exists to ask.
sources: [MODULE 4] A discovery call is a structured elicitation; translation; four questions; translation table; The discovery call that turned into a design session; Glossary – requirement vs. assumption · [MODULE 2] The scoping call that skipped the constraints
narration:
### Discovery reveals whether you're solving the right problem
By this stage you know how to evaluate a pattern, a platform and a control posture. Module four says discovery is what tells you whether you're solving the right problem at all. Stakeholders describe problems in business terms. Your job is to hear what the business is trying to achieve, find the constraints hidden inside that description, and leave the call with a record the design can follow.

### Listen, translate, write down
The module frames discovery as a three-step filter. First, listen to the business goal in plain language, and pay attention to the meaning behind the words. Stakeholders usually describe the outcome they want, not the constraint you need to design for. Second, translate it into requirements, assumptions and unresolved constraints, so you can see what the design must support, what still needs confirming, and what could block the solution later. Third, write those items down before the conversation moves on. Without that filter, the design inherits your assumptions, and the mismatch may not surface until changing course is slow and expensive.

### Preferences hide constraints
Translation is the core skill. Stakeholders speak in preferences; design decisions are made against constraints. Suppose a stakeholder says, we want this to feel seamless. If you write down seamless as the requirement, you haven't learned enough to design anything. The real work starts with the next question: what would make it feel not seamless? Maybe the user should never wait more than a second or two for the next step. Maybe they shouldn't re-enter information that already exists upstream. Maybe exceptions should move quietly to a human reviewer instead of showing a technical error. Maybe the workflow must stay inside one application. Each answer sharpens the design, and seamless becomes a latency target, an integration requirement, a handoff rule and a safe failure path.

So when you hear an experience word like seamless, easy, fast, simple or intuitive, treat it as a signal that more discovery is needed. Ask what would break the experience, what the user must never notice, what has to happen behind the scenes, and what must still be true when something goes wrong. A testable, bounded constraint is what you can build against.

### Four question categories
Force every vague statement into four buckets. What the system must do: capabilities as business outcomes, which is also where you separate Claude's work from existing systems and humans. What the system must not do: boundaries, prohibited actions, and cases that must route to a human. Stakeholders rarely volunteer these, so ask explicitly. What the system must cost: the budget constraint in terms the stakeholder controls, such as a latency target, a per-interaction cost ceiling or a volume forecast. And what the system must prove: the evidence the deployment must produce. In regulated workflows, proof obligations are part of the requirement set, and finding them in discovery is far cheaper than in a legal review weeks later.

### The translation table
Each item becomes one row: the stakeholder statement as said, the constraint it implies, the architectural decision that constraint forces, and any assumption you're documenting until it's confirmed. The module's examples. We want this to feel seamless implies a latency budget and failures that don't expose internals; the decision is a p95 target and a graceful failure state; the assumption is that seamless means perceived responsiveness and continuity, to be confirmed. It just needs to read the form and route it implies routing may be a deterministic business rule; the decision is to keep routing in the rule engine while Claude extracts; the assumption is that routing logic is owned outside the model, so confirm the owner. Clinicians will review the output anyway implies a licensed human must authorise output before it becomes part of a record with legal, financial or clinical consequence; the decision is a mandatory human authorisation gate; confirm authority and timing. We're in healthcare, so be careful with data implies a proof obligation under a health-privacy regime; the decision is audit-trail and data-handling evidence from day one; confirm scope with compliance.

The glossary adds a distinction worth remembering. A requirement traces to something the stakeholder actually said. An assumption is something the design takes for granted that was never stated. An unsourced assumption is the most dangerous kind, because nobody remembers deciding it.

### The discovery call that became a design session
The module's case: a regional hospital network says nurses spend forever writing up patient interactions and wants Claude to draft the clinical note from dictation. The architect replies that it's a clean augmented pattern, draft the note and write it back, prototype next week. The stakeholder agrees, mentioning there's a review step somewhere, just a quick check. Two weeks later, compliance finds three things. The quick check was a requirement that a licensed clinician authorise any model output before it reached the record, a mandatory architectural gate. The dictation contained protected health information that went through the context window without the required handling. And the network spanned two states with different record-retention rules that a single-region design ignored. None was unusual or hard to find. Each would have come from a must-prove or must-not-do question. The sketch was plausible, and plausibility is what made it dangerous: a stakeholder who hears a confident architecture assumes the questions were answered.

### The economics
A discovery call long enough to find the real constraints is far cheaper than redesigning after a hidden constraint emerges in legal or compliance review. The expensive failure is the unstated constraint that survives into production review, when changing the design costs the most.

## Part 6.2 – Communicate architectural decisions and trade-offs
minutes: 9
concepts: Make the decision possible, don't deliver a verdict; gain, give up, reversal cost, compliance posture; decision package (options, criteria, recommendation, remaining risks); Description competency for stakeholders; trade-off translation map; objection categories (capability, governance, design-choice); partner-track demo material not tested
example: A CTO approved a full-context design after hearing "about four cents per interaction". Six weeks later the invoice showed a five-figure monthly line. Nobody had multiplied by volume, raised caching for the static policy document, or said what unwinding full-context would cost once the system depended on it.
decision: Present every option with what it gains, what it gives up, what reversing it would cost once the system is built around it, and its effect on compliance posture, as a package the stakeholder can defend upward.
takeaway: An accurate presentation can still answer the wrong question. Name the reversal cost every time, especially when the design looks obviously simpler.
sources: [MODULE 4] Tradeoffs & GTM – your job is to make decisions possible; decision frame; decision package; Description; trade-off translation map; objection categories; The approval that was not an informed choice · [MODULE 1] Entry point key takeaway (name the trade-off out loud) · Partner-track demo and joint-scoping material is excluded from scope per the module's own labels
narration:
### Your job is to make the decision possible
Discovery told you what the buyer needs. Module four says the next job is turning those requirements into a decision a stakeholder can make. Your job isn't to resolve the trade-off before the meeting. It's to describe the options clearly enough that the stakeholder can make an informed choice they can defend later. Every meaningful decision has a trade-off: one option cuts cost or complexity but adds latency, risk or compliance burden; another improves experience or scale but needs more upfront effort. If you present only the conclusion, the decision looks clear but is weak, and when the downside appears, the stakeholder feels they approved something they didn't understand.

### The decision frame
Use four questions. What do we gain? What do we give up? What happens if we choose this now and have to reverse it later? And in regulated environments, what does this do to our compliance posture? The third question is the one most people skip, and it's often the one that changes the meeting. It moves the discussion from what's the better technical answer to what's the better business choice.

Technical precision is necessary but not sufficient. The architect may speak in latency, logging depth, context size, retrieval pattern and deployment route, while the executive is asking what happens to the business if we choose wrong. At that point the room needs translation, not more detail.

### A package, not a verdict
Present the decision as a package: the options you considered, the criteria you weighed them against, your recommendation, and the risks that remain. The stakeholder isn't adopting your architecture. They're accepting a decision they'll have to defend to their own leadership.

### Description for stakeholders
Description, the AI Fluency competency of telling an audience precisely what it needs, applies here. Frame the system's behaviour, limits and oversight in the stakeholder's terms, matched to their AI familiarity, without losing accuracy. The module gives three rules. Lead with the business outcome, not the architecture. Frame limitations honestly, because a security stakeholder trusts a system whose limits are clearly stated. And anticipate the peer-proof demand: the stakeholder will have to justify the choice to others, so they should leave with that justification in hand.

### The trade-off translation map
The module's map shows the shift from architecture framing to decision language. A larger context window per call versus retrieval over chunks: you gain a simpler design with the full document in view and fewer moving parts; you give up higher per-call cost and slower responses as volume grows, though caching can recover much of the input cost for static content held in context, so evaluate it before treating per-call cost as fixed; and a wrong, reversed choice costs reworking the architecture after a cost spike, plus the credibility hit of explaining an avoidable surprise. Trading logging detail for lower latency: you gain faster perceived response; you give up visibility into each interaction; reversed late, in a regulated workload, it's a potential compliance gap needing remediation. A single delivery route versus multi-platform: you gain lower build complexity and one auth and logging profile; you give up flexibility for regional, compliance or procurement needs; reversal can mean a delayed or blocked cutover if a late residency requirement emerges.

### The approval that wasn't an informed choice
The module's case. The architect recommends the larger context window so the full policy document stays in view, simpler and no retrieval layer. The CTO asks about cost per call. About four cents per interaction, and if the policy document is static, caching could bring the input portion down. The CTO approves. Six weeks later, on the invoice, the CTO writes: I approved a direction, not a number. Nobody told me four cents times our call volume was a five-figure monthly line. If the document was static, why weren't we caching it? And if we were going to build around full context, I needed to know what unwinding it would cost once the system depended on it. The presentation named the gain and answered the question as asked. It never named the reversal cost at production volume, which was the load-bearing element.

### Handling objections
Technical objections fall into three categories, each needing a different response. Capability objections ask whether the system can do the thing at all; answer with evidence. Governance and compliance objections ask whether it can be trusted, controlled and evidenced; answer with controls and artifacts. Design-choice objections ask why you chose this over another option; answer with the trade-off and what the alternative would have cost, using the same translation structure.

### What's out of scope
Module four also covers scenario-specific demos, limit placement in demos, working with the sales team and joint scoping with Anthropic's Applied AI team. The module labels those parts as partner track, not tested by the architect exam. The guide keeps them as optional reading. For the exam, focus on the decision frame, the package and the reversal cost.

## Part 6.3 – Manage stakeholder feedback loops and expectation alignment (including SLAs)
minutes: 10
concepts: Drift without active monitoring; feedback loop above observability (signals, triage, decide, act, review); SLA: what is measured, what counts as a breach, what happens; thresholds traced to UX, criticality, eval criteria; cost expectations at production volume; scheduled regulated checkpoints; governance table; iterate vs. re-architect
example: Over ninety days the eval score drifted down from week four while the error rate stayed flat, so no alert fired and no review was held. The stakeholder noticed in week eight and the quarterly review surfaced it in week twelve. A quality-drift trigger would have escalated by week five.
decision: Build a governance table before launch that maps each signal, including slow drifts and scheduled regulatory checks, to a trigger, an architect action and an owner, and trace every SLA threshold to its source.
takeaway: Monitoring isn't a feedback loop. A drift with no trigger is invisible until a human happens to notice.
sources: [MODULE 4] The feedback loop decides which signals reach a stakeholder; SLA; cost expectations; scheduled regulated checkpoints; production-signal governance table; The observability stack that replaced the feedback loop · iterate vs. re-architect guidance expands the governance-table row [INFERENCE]
narration:
### A live deployment drifts
Module four describes how a launched system slowly degrades. Picture a support assistant that starts in solid shape: fast, on tone, handling common questions well. Over time usage patterns change, new prompt styles appear and customer issues get more complex. Some responses slow down; some miss the mark. Nothing breaks dramatically, which is what makes drift hard to catch. Quality erodes gradually, and a team without a feedback loop may not see it until users already feel it.

### The decision layer above observability
Observability gives you the raw material: latency, error rates, eval scores, usage patterns. But a signal isn't a decision. One spike is noise; another is a real problem; a third matters only if it repeats. The feedback loop sits on top and answers five questions. Signals: what is the system showing us? Triage: what needs attention now, and what can wait? Decide: does this need a team fix, a stakeholder review or no action? Act: what correction, guardrail update or escalation is required? Review: did the response work, and does the rule need to change? The module's analogy is a train station control room. Sensors say which trains are late; someone still decides whether a delay is minor, whether passengers need telling, and whether the schedule must change.

### What an SLA names
Once the loop decides something matters, the SLA defines what happens when it crosses the line. An SLA makes three things clear: what we're measuring, what counts as a breach, and what happens when a breach occurs. The threshold should never be arbitrary. Latency should reflect the user-experience expectation found in discovery. Availability should reflect how critical the deployment is to the business. Quality should reflect the eval results and acceptance criteria already set. If a number can't be traced to one of those, it's probably a target someone chose because it sounded reasonable.

### Cost is the expectation that breaks most often
The module singles out cost. Production volume routinely runs one to two orders of magnitude above the pilot, so a cost that looked trivial in the proof of concept becomes a five-figure monthly line at scale. Get ahead of it. Give the stakeholder a consumption forecast at expected production volume, name the spend-control posture, such as caching, model tiering and budget alerts, and frame the model-tiering story before the first invoice, not after.

### Scheduled checkpoints in regulated deployments
Observability records what happened; the loop decides what to do. In regulated deployments, some reviews must happen even when nothing has gone wrong. A healthcare workflow with a documentation obligation may require periodic output audits on a schedule. A data-residency deployment may need scheduled confirmation that the environment still meets residency rules. These are design-time obligations. If they aren't built early, they become much more expensive to establish when someone asks for proof.

### The governance table
Build a table before launch that maps each signal to its trigger, the architect's response, and any regulatory checkpoint. The module's rows. Output quality, the eval score: the trigger is crossing the threshold drawn from the eval suite; the action is diagnosing whether the cause is prompt, data or model drift and deciding whether to iterate or re-architect; the regulated checkpoint is a periodic output audit against the documentation standard, on schedule, regardless of score. Latency p95: the trigger is crossing the budget set from user-experience requirements; the action is to find the bottleneck, then tune it, or escalate to a stakeholder review if the budget itself is wrong; usually no regulated checkpoint, unless latency is masking a logging or traceability gap. Cost per interaction: the trigger is crossing the budget agreed in discovery; the action is to identify the driver and bring a trade-off to the stakeholder if the budget needs revisiting. Data-residency configuration: the trigger is a scheduled confirmation; the action is to confirm and record residency posture and flag any drift immediately.

### When observability replaced the feedback loop
The module's trace shows ninety days side by side. Weeks one to three: eval score steady at baseline, launch review held. Weeks four to seven: the eval score drifts down week over week, but the error rate is flat, so no hard alert fires, and no review is scheduled. Weeks eight to twelve: the score keeps falling, the stakeholder says output is less useful lately, and the quarterly review finally surfaces it in week twelve. By design, the loop would have caught it seven weeks earlier. Every metric was collected. What was missing was a governance rule mapping slow quality drift to a review trigger. The stack was measuring the right thing and telling no one it mattered.

### Iterate or re-architect
The governance table's quality row ends in a decision: iterate or re-architect. Iterate when the cause is local, such as a prompt, a dataset or a threshold, and the architecture still fits the constraints. Re-architect when the constraint itself has moved, like volume growing an order of magnitude or a new residency rule, or when fixes keep failing. Escalate to the stakeholder when the budget or SLA is what's wrong.

## Part 6.4 – Document architectures and provide implementation guidance
minutes: 8
concepts: Three readers (handoff recipient, compliance reviewer, returning architect); decisions plus rejected alternatives and the trade-off each resolved; control register carried forward; dated decisions; labelled assumptions; open items with owners; completeness test; checklist fields; boundary conditions; runbooks and escalation paths
example: A financial-services replacement architect inherited a diagram with no rationale, switched context strategies to fix a performance issue, and reintroduced a data-handling pattern that broke the residency rule the original strategy had been chosen to satisfy.
decision: Document every decision with its date, the rejected alternatives and the trade-off it resolved, carry the control register forward with owners and evidence, and label assumptions as assumptions.
takeaway: The test: can a competent architect who wasn't in the room make a safe change after reading it? A diagram shows what the system is, not why.
sources: [MODULE 4] Documentation – three readers; documentation completeness checklist; The design rationale that lived in the Architect's head; Glossary – decision log, documentation completeness · [MODULE 5] Runbooks and escalation paths · [MODULE 2] Boundary conditions into the statement of work
narration:
### Documentation keeps the system working after you leave
The feedback loop keeps a system healthy while you're running it. Module four says documentation is what keeps it working after you're gone. Either the design carries its own reasoning into the handoff, or that reasoning disappears when you leave.

### One document, three readers
Architecture documentation serves three readers, and a document built for only one is incomplete even if it's detailed.

The handoff recipient inherits a deployment they had no part in building. For them, the document must carry the decisions made, the alternatives rejected, and why each was rejected. A design delivered without its rejected alternatives can't be understood by someone who wasn't in the room. They'll reverse the right decision for the wrong reason, or defend the wrong decision because they can't tell which trade-off it was resolving. The rejected options explain why the design is shaped the way it is.

The compliance reviewer arrives later looking for evidence that a specific control is live and accounted for. For them, the document must carry each regulatory obligation, the technical control that satisfies it, the owner, and the evidence artifact showing it operates. That's the control register from episode five, carried forward into the living document that governs production. Reviewers don't accept assertions; a statement that a control exists isn't enough.

The returning architect, often you, comes back months later with no memory of the design sessions. For them, the document must stand alone. Decisions are dated. Assumptions are labelled as assumptions, not embedded as facts. Open items have owners and resolution criteria.

### The completeness test
The test is practical: after reading the document, can a competent architect who wasn't in the design sessions make a safe change to the system? If not, it isn't complete.

### The checklist
The module's completeness checklist has six fields. Decision: the architectural choice and its date, for all three readers. Rejected alternatives: options considered but not chosen, mainly for the handoff recipient. Trade-off named: what the decision resolved in terms of gains, costs and reversal implications, for the recipient and the returning architect. Owner: who is responsible for the decision or control going forward, for the reviewer and the recipient. Evidence artifact: what demonstrates a control is actually operating, for the reviewer. And audit-ready status: whether the evidence is current and sufficient for review.

### The rationale that lived in one head
The module's postmortem is from financial services. The original architect left twelve weeks after launch. The replacement inherited a thorough architecture diagram with no rationale attached. A performance issue prompted a switch of context strategy, and the switch reintroduced a data-handling pattern that broke the deployment's data-residency constraint. At launch the original architect had designed that context strategy specifically to keep regulated data in-region. At handoff, nothing recorded why. The replacement was competent and acted reasonably on what they had. The diagram told them what the system was, not why, so they couldn't tell the strategy was load-bearing for compliance. If it's never written down, it leaves with you.

### Implementation guidance
Documentation also guides the people building and running the system. Module two says boundary conditions go into the statement of work, so the build team and the business owner agree on what the system handles and what's out of scope. Module five adds the operational side: a runbook capturing known symptom-to-cause-to-action paths, and an escalation path naming who handles what and when an issue leaves the team. Together with the decision log, those let a team change and operate the system safely without you.

### Cost, complexity, risk
Writing rationale and evidence at design time takes time; reconstructing it later from email threads, or failing to, costs a wrong reversal in production. The discipline is recording why, not just what, and labelling assumptions, which is easy to skip when the reasoning feels obvious to the person who lived it. The costly failure is a successor who reverses a load-bearing decision and reintroduces a violation the original design had solved.

narration:
### The lifecycle
Module four runs a lifecycle under all its topics: discovery, design, handoff, monitoring and iteration. Discovery and trade-off framing do the discovery and design work. The feedback loop is the monitoring and iteration phase. Documentation is the handoff phase. And entry-point selection with the outcome document closes the loop. The module's point is that identifying which phase a decision belongs to is what lets you judge when a phase is ready to move to the next.

### What each phase must leave behind
Each phase produces the artifact the next depends on. Discovery leaves the translation table: requirements, constraints and labelled assumptions. Design leaves the architecture plus the trade-off package the stakeholder approved, with reversal costs named, and the sizing and feasibility verdict from module two. Handoff leaves the decision log with rejected alternatives, the control register with owners and evidence, runbooks and an escalation path. Monitoring leaves the governance table, the SLAs traced to their sources, and the scheduled regulated checkpoints. Iteration leaves eval-gated changes and an updated record. If a phase ends without its artifact, the next phase builds on assumption.

### Moving from proof of concept to production
Module two shows the lifecycle's most dangerous transition. A POC proves capability at low volume on clean inputs with a patient user. Production runs at real volume, on real inputs, with impatient users. Cost, latency, reliability and failure modes are invisible in the demo, so they must be designed in before the move: a cost model from the token distribution, p95 targets, retries with backoff, circuit breakers and fallback chains, and failure modes named per architecture. Model version pinning applies throughout: pin versions, watch the deprecation page and keep a version-update runbook, so iteration doesn't happen by accident.

### Entry point, revisited with the full production picture
Module four returns to route selection with the system live. The earlier question was which route survives the compliance filter. Now it's which route performs best on latency, cost and compliance for a live multi-platform deployment. Cross-platform deployments expose problems a single route never shows. Model identifier strings differ across routes. Feature availability can lag on cloud-provider routes compared with the direct API. Regional availability on Bedrock and Vertex needs explicit configuration, and defaulting to a global endpoint is the common pattern that breaks a residency requirement. The module notes that because entry-point capabilities change, specific claims should be re-verified at build time.

The decision matrix summarises it. The direct Anthropic API gets the newest features first with the fewest extra hops; it's the default unless procurement or residency points elsewhere, but confirm coverage by configuration. AWS Bedrock and GCP Vertex AI are region-configurable with possible feature lag, and fit partners standardised on those clouds who need in-region execution, when configured explicitly. Microsoft Foundry varies by hosting form: models hosted on Azure run inference in the partner's Azure environment, while models hosted on Anthropic infrastructure route there, so verify residency per route and don't assume it from the platform name.

### The entry-point-responsibility map
An application that uses several Claude entry points in one workflow must say which entry point handles which task and why. The module's example: the API for back-end inference, Claude Code for an engineering sub-task, and a Bedrock endpoint for a regulated data path. Each boundary is an integration point with its own authentication, logging and failure profile. The map, written before integration code, prevents the common failure where an entry point chosen for one task quietly takes on another because the routing was never documented.

### Closing the loop with the outcome document
The outcome document turns the deployment's results into something people outside the build can understand. Its six fields: the use case with scope boundary, the metric before, the metric after on the same definition, the control that makes the comparison auditable, the measurement owner, and the reuse potential. The module labels the reuse field as partner-track content; the rest is on the blueprint. Technical metrics alone don't make the document. The before-and-after business outcome does.

### The cumulative brief
The module ends with a brief that walks the whole lifecycle. A regional healthcare network across two states deploys a clinical documentation assistant. Nurses dictate, Claude drafts the structured note, and a licensed clinician authorises every note before it reaches the record. There's a health-privacy obligation with an audit trail, and a data-residency rule. The partner is standardised on AWS but runs non-regulated back-end work on the direct API. The original architect is rotating off, and four weeks in, the CFO wants to know what it's worth. Work it phase by phase. Discovery: the four categories, especially must prove and must not do. Design: an authorisation gate, PHI handling, in-region routing on Bedrock for the regulated path, and an entry-point-responsibility map for the direct-API work. Handoff: a decision log with rejected alternatives and a control register. Monitoring: a governance table with scheduled residency and documentation checks. And closing: an outcome document with documentation time before and after, an auditable control, and an owner.
