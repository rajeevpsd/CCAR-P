## Scenario-specific demo design `[MODULE 4]`
A **capabilities demo** answers "what can this do?". A **scenario-specific demo** answers "what does it do with *my* problem?". Only the second builds confidence.

Four design decisions:

| Decision | What the architect decides | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario selection | A workflow the buyer recognises (their data shapes, approvals, edge cases) | Familiarity convinces more than polish |
| Limit placement | Name one or two limits up front as deliberate scope boundaries | A limit the buyer discovers drops confidence; a stated one signals rigour (especially in regulated settings) |
| Sales collaboration | Shape the narrative with sales before building | Avoids answering unasked questions or overpromising |
| Data preparation | Realistic, anonymised data with the same structural constraints | The buyer judges the demo by the data |

Limit framing: what is the limit, why does it exist, and what happens if the use case needs to go beyond it?

## Technical objection handling `[MODULE 4]`
Three categories, each answered differently:
- **Capability:** can it do this at all?
- **Governance / compliance:** can it be trusted, controlled and evidenced?
- **Design choice:** why this and not that? Answer with gain, give-up and reversal cost.
